How to conduct an interview

Over the last two years I’ve been interviewed hundreds of times. Here are a few of the things I’ve learned, and apply when I conduct my own interviews.

1. Familiarize yourself with the subjects’ work.

The road to a bad interview is ignorance. Avoid this by learning as much about the interviewee as possible before approaching them. I’ll typically read as much as possible about a person before I contact them to ask questions.

2. Come up with an overall interview theme.

An interview needs to focus on one specific topic. Many questions I receive seem to have little or no connection with each other. Avoid this by picking an overarching theme for the interview.

3. Craft a few specific questions.

Interviews don’t need to have a million questions. A few good questions are all a subject needs.

Questions for your questions:

The next step in preparing an interview is asking questions of your questions. Here are typical questions I’ll ask before sending an interview.

1. Is this question relevant to the subjects current work?

No one likes getting asked about work they were doing five years ago. Ask about what they’re working on now.

2. Have I already decided/assumed what the answer will be?

If your cup is full, it leaves little room for the interviewee. Leave room in your question for an answer.

The ask

Finally, when you’re asking for an interview with a subject, it’s important to respect their time and focus. Here are a few things to give up-front when requesting an interview.

1. Ask permission to send interview questions.

Many people are incredibly busy, and might not have time to answer interview questions. It’s important to ask whether it’s okay first, before sending questions. (You can ignore this for me, because I’ve asked you to interview me.)

2. State the interview theme upfront.

What is the over-arching theme in the interview? Make this clear, concise, and easily understandable so that the interview can thrive within a container.

3. When do you need the interview by?

Next week, next year? Deadlines are a good way to get things done quickly, so no one forgets about the interview.

4. Let them know when you’re planning to publish the interview.

Example: Next tuesday, I’d like to publish this interview. It’s best to publish interviews quickly after you’ve interviewed. The world is moving faster, waiting three months can change a lot of things.

5. Who will read the interview?

Do you have an audience? Who are they? How many are there? These are good things to provide to anyone you’re trying to interview — especially when you begin moving into interviewing micro-celebrities and other people who’ve been working hard on their craft.
