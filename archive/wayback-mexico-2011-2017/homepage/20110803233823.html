    Written by Ev Bogue | Follow me on Google+

    Everyone’s good at talking right? But we’re not all good at writing.

    A few months ago, I was at a talk Seth Godin gave in Seattle, and he said something that continues to resonate with me.

    “It’s not hard for me to blog everyday, because I write like I talk.”

    I’m taking this information and attempting to integrate it into my work.

    It’s tempting to constantly be pushing the edge in the world of writing. However, what I’ve found is that when it comes to blogging, it’s easier for me to get a post out every day if I don’t. Instead, I relax around the words. I don’t try to be perfect.

    Instead? I do my best to speak from experience.

    What Every Movement Needs: an Idea

    Written by Ev Bogue | Follow me on Google+

    “Ideas in secret die. They need light and air or they starve to death.” – Seth Godin

    The idea is the most important part of any movement.

    A leader has to pick one actual idea and stick with it.

    Many people who set out to create movements avoid having ideas, and that’s why their movements either don’t lift off the ground or fail.

    We avoid ideas to be safe. Movements are never safe.

    We may feel that we have to make our idea apply to the broadest swath of the population as possible. This belief has been embedded into our psyches by years of education, work, and socialization.

    An idea must be focused, it must be powerful, it must be specific.

    Ideally, you should be able to condense the idea into one word or phrase.

    Anyone who sees your movement must be able to make up their mind in less than 10 seconds. Are you interested in joining or too afraid? Go!

    Most important, your idea must leave people out. Not everyone can join your movement, and you don’t want them too. If your idea is broad enough to apply to everyone, it’s also too broad to have any meaning.

    How to Untether from Your Music Collection

    Written by Ev Bogue | Follow me on Google+

    Two years ago, I took a crate full of CDs, stuck a note on them “shit that was weighing down Everett’s life” and put them in the hall for my roommates to MP3 and/or recycle. I was done with physical CDs.

    Now, I’m ready to do that with my MP3 collection. Yes. Delete all of my MP3s, and never own music again.

    Why? Because I’ve been playing with Spotify (which has a free version). $9.99 gets me access to every song that I ever wanted to download. That Smashing Pumpkins single that I’ve been keeping on my external hard drive since Napster because I never thought anyone would ever be able to find it again? I don’t need to keep it, store it, and think about playing it again anymore.

    Yes, after a quick search I found that I can even play cheesy teenage emo power pop that I’ve never grown out of like Kill Hannah. Yes, there are some things I haven’t been able to untether from.

    Because all of the music I listen to now lives in the cloud, I don’t have to dedicate brain or hard drive space to maintaining it. Hurray!

    Obviously there are other ways to stream music over the Internet like Pandora. Also, we can buy anything we want for almost nothing at the Apple Store. But nothing seems to (currently) beat randomly remembering a song that I used to listen to, searching, and then playing it within 5 seconds.

    //

    Every once in awhile the Internet jumps ahead, it’s an evolutionary leap. If I want to keep up, I have to jump ahead with it.

    With infinite copies, ownership becomes a burden. Sharing becomes the norm. With decentralized costs for music.

    A good video about this is Sam Lessin’s TED talk on the end of ownership.

    //

    This is a similar distribution model to Zipcar. I’ve used their service dozens over the last two years. In Seattle, there are two Zipcars in the parking lot behind my apartment. All I have to do is log on with my mobile app, select the Zipcar behind my house (if it’s available) and I’m heading to the airport to pick up Gwen Bell.

    Easy, huh?

    When we share resources, everything in the world gets cheaper and easier.

    Unlearning What The Internet Wants

    Written by Ev Bogue | Follow me on Google+

    I have 5 years of conventional education.

    I dropped out of kindergarten.

    I didn’t go back after freshman year of high school.

    Eventually, I graduated from NYU in three years with two majors.

    Now I’m 26 years old, so effectively an adult created out of the loose system of unschooling. Basically: don’t tell me what to learn. I’ll decide what I want to learn, when I want to.

    Also, I’ll decide what I’ll unlearn too, when I want to.

    This is a new section on my site. Let’s call it: unlearning. Every week, until I decide to un-post schedule myself, I’ll be writing an intention to unlearn something for that week.

    //

    This week on unlearning…

    I’ve decided that I have no idea how the Internet works. So, I’ve decided to unlearn it.

    Whenever I think I’ve mastered something, chances are I’ve just achieved a false sense of entitlement. People with a false sense of entitlement I generally want to smack, unfollow, and put on my zombie wall.

    So, I declare: I do not know how the Internet works. I do not know what it wants. I do not know what it needs.

    //

    It’s time for some unlearning.

    Every time there’s a major evolution in the Internet world, it upends everything. When Livejournal came along 12 years ago, nothing before it mattered much any more. When Facebook came along 8 years ago, we all forgot there was an Internet at all.

    Now we’re living in a new age. It’s hard to put a finger on what changed about the Internet. It might be that everyone is viewing it from their phones, it might be HTML5 and CSS3 getting rolled out into the code everywhere, it might be Google+, it may be there is an entire generation of children running amuck on the networks who don’t know what the world looked like before the Internet.

    Whatever the new paradigm is, it’s time for some unlearning.

    So I set an intention. Or rather, an un-tention.

    Let’s erase everything I think about how the Internet worked before. It’s old knowledge.

    It’s time to reinvent how I experience the Internet.

    Will you join me?

    Comments + Publishing

    Written by Ev Bogue | Follow me on Google+

    Anil Dash recently wrote something that fundamentally changed the way I think about community on the web. “If your website is full of assholes, it’s your fault.”

    A year ago, after my digital sabbatical, I turned off comments on my internet presence in order to focus on my writing.

    Now, after my digital sabbatical, I’ve turned comments back on.

    Essentially, I took off an entire year to look internally for answers. Now I feel ready to interact more on the web.

    My intention is to grow a community around Evbogue.com while I’m investigating the work that matters to me.

    //

    I’d like you all to keep a few things in mind when you’re commenting.

    Evbogue.com is like my living room. My current living room in Seattle is also my bedroom. I roll out my futon when I want to go to sleep, I roll it up when I wake up. I roll out a yoga mat when I want to practice, then I roll it up and put it away when I’m done with it. When I have guests, we sit on the floor and enjoy tea and quiet conversation.

    I hope that the threads under my posts can have the same feeling of tranquility. If we’re taking a moment to challenge someone’s ideas, then let’s do it an civilized way.

    Whenever I leave a comment on someone else’s website, I leave it in a deliberate way. I carefully craft the message, because I know that many people will be reading it. I know that my comments all lead back to me — if I want to be taken seriously, I comment from an aligned place.

    When I’m triggered by someone’s work, I step away in order to breathe and practice before I respond in a reactionary way. It’s amazing how different my opinion about something changes after 3-5 deep breathes.

    //

    Because this site is my living room, I reserve the right to ask people who aren’t acting appropriately to leave.

    It’s tempting sometimes for me to try to push my own products on other people’s sites. But I do my best to resist these urges. I know that an intelligent comment on another website does more to sell my own stuff than pushing or self promotion in someone else’s living room.

    You’re all moderators here: If you notice a comment that seems out of place, feel free to flag it. It’ll drop me a note so I can moderate if necessary. If five of you flag a comment that seems out of place, it will disappear.

    I can’t spend all day moderating and responding my comments, because I’m a professional writer — I need to dedicate most of my day to creating work and exploring my world.

    A great book on how to communicate better, offline as well as only is What We Say Matters. This is a book I’ve been studying and implementing in my life in an active way.

    //
