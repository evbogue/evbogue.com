Operator’s Manual

In the beginning, we were all computer Operators.

On December 9th 1968, Douglas Engelbart gave a video recorded demo of a computer in San Francisco. This has come to be known as The Mother of All Demos. In his talk, which you can find on the Internet and watch, he refers to the person who manipulates the computer as The Operator. Now, I can’t tell you for certain, because I wasn’t there. I wasn’t born for another 17 years. But this video has led me to believe that in the beginning we were all supposed to be computer Operators.

But over time the idea of the computer Operator has been demonized and outlawed by almost every computer company known to man. The title of choice has grown to be known as the user. A user is a dim-witted mouse-clicking, screen thumbing nincompoop who is content to run his own mind through digital hamsterwheels for the rest of his life at his own expense while making some other smarter motherfucker richer.

Over time the idea of a user has transformed from a person who uses a computer to write Microsoft Word documents to a person who is being used by the computer. These days it’s not uncommon to see a human who has been possessed by his machine. This user is unable to distinguish reality from what his computer is telling him. The machine dictates his life, rather than him telling the machine what to do on his behalf.

This is horrible, because over time we’ve seen the dream of augmenting our humanity with computers be transformed into a living hell of people being used by proxy via their computers by evil masters living far far away. Meanwhile these masters–let’s from now on refer to them as douchebags–sit on giant piles of cash generated by manipulating user minds all over the world

Why has the Operator become demonized in favor of the much more compliant user? Because an Operator is a dangerous person to giant software companies owned by douchebags. The Operator knows how to manipulate a computer for his own ends. An Operator is an independent mind in a sea of compliant users. An Operator knows what is up, and isn’t going to take it anymore.

To illustrate this, we only have to talk about The Cloud. You know about The Cloud right? It’s a term that’s been shoved down your throat by the software companies owned by douchebags since the mid 2000s. Everyone knows about The Cloud, but what is it? Can you describe it? Can you Operate it? No. In fact, the more you think about The Cloud the more you realize that it’s only a marketing term used to trick the users into giving all of their data to giant storage centers, where that data can be used for any purposes the software companies see useful for their own ends.

And the more you think about The Cloud the more you realize that The Cloud is a big trick, perhaps one of the most epic mindfucks of all time, in an attempt to make sure no person on the planet figures out how to become an Operator. Because if they were to become an Operator they would realize that there is no way in hell they’d ever put their data in The Cloud.

Perhaps it might be best to refer to it as The Evil Cloud, so we can imagine this dark omninous cloud of evildoing sweeping over the entire planet, assimilating everyone’s brains into being pawns for the giant cash pile somewhere that no one gets access to except Evil Cloud software company owning douchebags.

This is where you come in. It’s time for you to learn how to be an Operator.

As you become an Operator you learn how to have freedom and autonomy while operating computer systems towards your own ends.

Because now is the moment when The Evil Cloud software companies are weakest and users everywhere are looking up from their phones and realizing that we have eight other digits besides our thumbs, and those digits must have evolved for something! And that something was learning how to Operate their machine. And so these users step off their digital hamster wheel, open up their computer screen and begin to poke around on their machine for the first time in ten years.

It turns out the Operator is an ancient profession, as old as computing itself. It’s only been lost to us as we’ve been manipulated into becoming users. The tools we needed to become an Operator have always been available to us. In fact, the whole time we were users the software was still being developed. Some of it was even being used to use us. However, once we discover that we are in fact computer Operators, and not users at all, we can realize that these computers do in fact have the infinite potential that Doug Engelbart dreamed of on that day he demoed the computer on December 9th 1968.

Are you ready to become an Operator in 2015?

Practical skills for Operators

“Okay! I’m so sick of being a user that I just went into the kitchen and puked into the sink. I want to become an operator! I buy this! But what practical skills will I learn?”

Okay. I’ll tell you.

The operator is one of the most ancient computing professions on the planet. There were Operators at Bell Labs in the 1960s! We’ve only forgotten how to be an Operator because those douchebags at the big tech companies wanted us to forget so we’d be used by their products.

The practical skills of being an Operator are rooted in the basics of Unix and The Internet. Once you understand how the Internet works, and how it’s a series of machines networked together in various ways, you’ll be started on your professional journey. Next you’ll need to get done some of the most basic of UNIX commands that are immortalized these days on the most popular operating system on the planet, Linux.

Inside Operator’s Manual you’ll learn…

  • How to obtain a virtual computer
  • How to operate your local machine
  • How to operate your shell (zsh, bash, wha?)
  • How to operate (Li)unix
  • How to secure your shell
  • How to do superuser operations under root and using sudo
  • How to choose and operate a distribution
  • How to operate your terminal
  • How to understand virtual and computer memory
  • What the hell is bandwidth and how will you ever use 1TB of it?
  • How to operate The Web from your terminal
  • The differences between operators, developers, programmers and designers
  • How to stay on the latest cutting edge Operating System release
  • How to install and operate free programs
  • How to operate systemd
  • How to operate identities
  • How to operate DNS and understand IP addresses
  • How to operate in the local/server abstraction
  • How to troubleshoot operating problems

All of these above topics are chapters within Operator’s Manual. However, I haven’t mentioned all of the chapters. For your reading pleasure and surprise, I’ve also included a few high-def opinionated chapters about such things as Dealing with Users, and How to Operate a Server Worth Visiting. There will probably be more, and you’ll have to read to discover them. These secret un-listed chapters don’t contain any practical skills at all, instead you’ll learn more about how to fill your shoes now that you’re an Operator.

In total Operator’s Manual contains 30 chapters, all of which are around 1000 words each, for a total of mas o menos 30,000 words. One of the things I want to talk about right are Operating Systems.

You see, we’ve forgotten all about Operating Systems. That’s because at some point a douchebag shortened the term to OS, so we’d forget that Operating Systems are for Operators. Operating Systems weren’t meant to be dumb, instead they were meant to be a gateway to your computer processor. Operating Systems were supposed to make it easier for you to reach the guts of the machine.

Perhaps we can differentiate Operating Systems from OSes. OSes are for people who only know how to use their thumb, whereas an Operating System is for Operators.

The most important element of an operating system is having access to a Terminal Emulator. From your Terminal Emulator you’ll be able to reach that unlimited computing potential that the Ancient Operators dreamed of.

Using a Terminal Emulator is hard. Your thumb will be best used to hit the spacebar over and over again. But you’ll also need your left pinky finger to hit the tab key to autocomplete commands using your shell. In fact, all of your fingers will be put to use as an Operator –this will keep them from falling off when you’re older from non-use.

Once you finish reading Operator’s Manual and practicing with your Terminal Emulator, you’ll have all of the basics down to become an Operator yourself. Then you’ll be able to start challenging yourself with more complex operations that I haven’t written about in this book.

If you’re looking for instructions on how to operate a web server, you’re in the wrong place. Be sure to check out my other book Minimum Viable Website. MVW ignores most of the important things about becoming an Operator, instead focusing on going from zero to getting a web server up and running on the distributed web. There’s some overlap, but one key difference. Operator’s Manual does not cover anything about getting a web server up and running. So if you want to get a web server up and running once you’ve become an Operator, you’ll need to buy MVW too.
